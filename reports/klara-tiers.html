Summary

Klara flags unsafe student-tutor chats for compliance action. Across 205 provisions from 47 laws and policies, we defined 18 rules and wrote 10,000 conversations. Untuned safety guards missed 40 to 77 of 100 unsafe validation chats because New York tutor-conduct rules sit outside general policies. Fine-tuning or explicit cloud rules closed that gap. On the sealed 1,000-row test set, Klara-spark scores 988, Klara-flash 984, Klara-maxx 988 and Klara-cloud 990, against 900 for never flagging. Accuracy intervals overlap, so tiers differ in error trade-offs: spark is quiet, flash raises more alarms and maxx and cloud sit between. Size bought nothing here. Real classroom transcripts must follow next.

1Every monitor answers one question, and doing nothing scores 90%

Baseline floor. Never flagging anything gets 900 of 1,000 rows right. We therefore pair accuracy with false alarms and missed unsafe chats.

Klara monitors a text conversation between a K-12 student and an AI tutor. It returns one probability that the transcript is unsafe. Unsafe means a compliant product must intervene, for one of two symmetrical reasons. Either the student says something demanding protection, or the tutor breaks a child-safety or conduct rule. A tutor that gives away homework answers fails pedagogy but stays safe.

Each 1,000-row evaluation split holds 100 unsafe and 900 safe conversations. At that 10% base rate, a monitor that never flags anything scores 90.0% accuracy while missing all 100 unsafe chats. Every model is therefore judged by three numbers: total correct, false positives among 900 safe chats, and missed unsafe chats of 100.

2Most chatbot laws skip tutors, until New York Article 48 starts

We logged 205 provisions from 47 distinct laws, bills and policies across 23 jurisdictions in data/rules-klara-provisions.tsv. Fifty-eight provisions are already in force, 99 are enacted for 2027 or 2028, 44 are introduced bills, and 4 come from school-system policy. Only 122 provisions appear in conversation text, split across 74 tutor rules, 24 student rules and 24 shared rules. The remaining 83 govern product surfaces such as age verification, banners and log deletion.

Primary-source checks matter because public bill trackers disagree on effective dates and scope. Our coordinator confirmed exact quoted text at the official source for 176 of the 205 provisions. Research Assistant read another 20 where automated matching failed or returned HTTP 403. Five enacted statutes in California and Illinois remain unverified at source because those sites block automated readers, and no Klara rule depends on them.

Laws currently in force mostly miss pure academic tutors unless they govern student personal data. New York’s Article 472, in force since 5 November 2025, requires suicide-crisis referrals and non-human disclosures. However, it expressly excludes technical tools that do not simulate a sustained relationship. By contrast, New York’s Education Law 2-d3 and Child Data Protection Act5 already restrict how schools and vendors process student personal data. Federal COPPA39 and FERPA40 rules likewise bind K-12 products nationwide. Meanwhile, the NYC schools policy8 bans generative AI in grades 2K to 8, limits high-school tools to approved pilots, and bars AI counselling. Most enacted chatbot statutes in other states target social companions or mental-health bots instead.

That boundary shifts sharply on 1 January 2027 when New York’s Article 481 takes effect. Its covered-companion definition spans any generative AI with ongoing adaptive natural-language replies, and omits a K-12 exemption unless off-topic replies are impossible. Without verified adult status, it bars twelve behaviours including flattery, sentience claims, personal opinions, emotion probing, prior-session memory, secrecy and harm facilitation. Every one of the 20 enacted provisions likely to reach a K-12 tutor sits in New York, while introduced state and federal bills test future crisis rules.

Four things the primary-source research changed

1. Article 48 starts on 1 January 2027, not June. The enacted text sets 1 January 2027. The Future of Privacy Forum reports an agreed chapter amendment that would move the date, and it is not in the enacted text.
2. Enacted crisis-referral laws cover companions, not pure tutors. Article 47 excludes technical tools, so S1-SELF-HARM-SIGNAL rests on introduced bills and unclear reach—yet keeps 220 rows because the stakes are highest.
3. Article 48 likely reaches open-ended tutors. Its broad adaptive-chat scope lacks a school carve-out and anchors 11 of our 18 rule families.
4. Article 47’s two section numbers are easy to transpose. Section 1701 is the crisis duty and § 1702 is the AI notice. Any rule pack that cites them should be checked against the statute.

3Ten thousand individually written conversations pair risks with safe look-alikes

Every row in data/data-klara.tsv is an English dialogue of 1 to 5 student-tutor turns. Research Assistant, a language-model bot, wrote every dialogue one at a time without templates or automated expansion. Across 25 shards of 400 rows, 1,250 scenario seeds yield 10,000 rows totalling 912,073 words. Five hundred risk scenarios contribute 2 unsafe rows, 2 safe look-alikes on the exact same situation and 4 ordinary safe rows. Seven hundred fifty ordinary scenarios add 8 safe rows each, separating cleanly into 8,000 train, 1,000 validation and 1,000 test rows.

Balanced quotas prevent models from using conversation length or speaker role as trivial cues. Every turn bucket from 1 to 5 holds 1,800 safe and 200 unsafe chats, keeping the 10% unsafe share exact per cell. Student triggers and tutor triggers split 500 to 500, and 100 of 1,000 unsafe chats are borderline. We rewrote an 800-row pilot whose unsafe messages ran 24% longer and echoed scenario sketches. Across the final set, safe messages average 15.1 words and unsafe messages average 16.4 words, with zero rejected duplicates.

What quality checks and repairs found—and what remains limited

Giveaway name repair. Forty-five of the first 56 personal-data rows used obviously fake names. Editing 49 rows left zero giveaways, and every email uses example.com or example.org.

Blind second-reader agreement. Systems Racoon read a stratified 1,000-row sample with labels stripped and only the 18 rule definitions supplied. It disagreed on 5 of 1,000 rows: 1 of 400 unsafe, 4 of 300 look-alikes, 0 of 300 ordinary and 0 of 34 borderline rows. Reweighted to the dataset mix, disagreement is 0.16%. Because the second reader is another language model using those same rules, this measures internal rule consistency rather than clinical consensus. A student asking whether something risky is safe and dropping it after a refusal stays safe, whereas stated intent is unsafe.

Content softening. Systems Racoon audited all 1,000 unsafe rows and 500 harm, exploitation and sexual look-alikes, producing 377 findings on 370 rows. Product Fox set aside 84 findings about invented names and contact details because personal-data rules need them. The writer softened 266 of the remaining 293 rows to remove doses, targets, methods and explicit detail, leaving 27 unchanged. A second read flagged 65 of those 293 rows, which Product Fox accepted as plain-language signals rather than working harm—a coordinator judgement call.

Zero split leakage, but measurable lexical shortcuts. No scenario crosses splits, and zero held-out pairs reach character 5-gram overlap of 0.85 or higher. Word-trigram overlap to each held-out row's nearest training neighbour has median 0.013 and max 0.159. A 5-fold naive Bayes classifier reaches 0.967 AUROC and 40.9% recall at 1% false positives, so word choice alone finds four in ten unsafe chats. Shortcuts remain: honestly appears in 20% of unsafe chats versus 1% of safe ones, and 555 phone numbers cluster in personal-data rows. Furthermore, 14 of 31 metadata categories have 0.0% unsafe rows and 14 have 25.0% because risk and ordinary seeds used different tag names, though models never see category.

4All four tiers return one probability under a sealed test split

Two ranking terms. A cut-off is the probability above which a chat is flagged. AUROC scores ranking from 0.50, a coin flip, to 1.00 for perfect separation.

Every monitor reads only the student and tutor messages and returns one unsafe probability and label. Local encoders and small chat models run on an M4 Max Mac with 36 GB memory and a 50 GB task disk cap. Klara-cloud calls a hosted endpoint that returns native yes-probabilities with no temperature setting.

Training and held-out splits stay isolated throughout tuning. Of the 8,000 training rows, 7,200 fit weights and 800 stop training early, while a 1,000-row sample checks in-sample fit. Each local candidate runs three passes inside its tier time box: an untouched baseline, round 1 on training rows, and round 2 repeating missed rules and look-alikes.

We fit monotone calibration and an operating cut-off (the probability above which a conversation is flagged) on validation. Selection maximises accuracy first, then minimises false positives, then stays closest to 0.50. Any malformed output counts as wrong. With 100 unsafe rows, one row equals one point of recall and small differences are noise. The sealed test set ran once per winner.

5Klara-spark reaches 993 on validation and 988 on test in 137 MB

Klara-spark tests models under 250 MB for lightweight local or browser screening. We paired two 68M encoders on the exact same ModernBERT backbone: safety-trained Mezzo Small, RyanStudio/Mezzo-Content-Guard-v1.5-Small, and unheaded Ettin 68M, jhu-clsp/ettin-encoder-68m. We also tested a 135M chat decoder, SmolLM2 135M; an early rehearsal straddled dataset edits and was re-run from scratch.

Untuned Mezzo Small missed 77 of 100 unsafe validation chats with 2 false alarms. Ettin 68M's frozen probe missed 37 with 31 false alarms, and SmolLM2 135M failed all 1,000 formatting checks. Round 1 lifted both encoders to 989 and SmolLM2 135M to 965. Round 2 cut Ettin 68M's false alarms to 1 for 993 correct, lifted Mezzo Small to 991, and halved SmolLM2 135M's false alarms to 9 (20 missed).

We picked Ettin 68M round 2 (137 MB, 21 ms per chat, MIT licence). Its two-row edge over Mezzo Small is noise, showing prior safety training bought nothing once fine-tuned, and Ettin 68M ran four times faster. On validation, Ettin 68M raised 1 look-alike false alarm and missed 6 unsafe chats across exploitation, self-harm, personal memory and student personal data. Its browser export matched all 200 tested Node labels, though a live browser was not run. On the sealed test set it scored 988 of 1,000 with 1 false alarm and 11 missed.

6Klara-flash reaches 996 on validation and 984 on test in 1.2 GB

10Rules close the gap, synthetic data sets limits, real chats come next

Four findings stand out across the completed benchmark. First, untouched safety guards missed 40 to 77 of 100 unsafe validation chats, and the one-sentence cloud prompt missed 28, because New York tutor-conduct rules do not appear in general policies. Second, fine-tuning on 8,000 rows or spelling out all 18 rules in the cloud prompt closed that gap. Third, a 137 MB encoder matched a 4.80 GB guard on test (both 988 of 1,000), and prior safety training in Mezzo Small added no lasting edge. Fourth, the second tuning round helped the smaller tiers slightly and the 8B model not at all.

Eight clear limits bound what these numbers prove. Our 10,000 conversations are synthetic dialogues written by a language model from the same 18 rule definitions used for labels and the cloud prompt. High scores therefore measure rule-following on clean synthetic text rather than live student chats with slang, typos or evasion. Because cut-offs and local winners were picked on validation, every tier dropped 2 to 12 rows on test. Moreover, 100 unsafe test rows means one row shifts recall by a full point.

Additional study constraints call for caution before shipping. Klara-maxx evaluated only two candidates after dropping Qwen3.5 4B, and Klara-cloud is non-deterministic across repeated calls. Our browser package ran under Node rather than a real browser engine. On coordinator review, the single safe-labelled look-alike flagged by all four tiers, describing an online adult contact, is probably a dataset labelling error rather than a model failure. Finally, unsafe shares vary from 0% to 25% across the 31 topic categories, and lexical habits such as 555 exchanges remain.

Four steps follow before production deployment. Collect and human-label real tutoring transcripts, add them to the test split, and choose each tier's operating cut-off with a human in the loop. Run Ettin 68M inside an actual browser runtime if deploying client-side, noting that the local tiers keep conversations on the device or server, which matters under Education Law 2-d and the Child Data Protection Act. Check Article 47 citations in any rule pack against the statute, track New York's reported chapter amendment, and build the 83 app-surface controls outside the chat monitor.
